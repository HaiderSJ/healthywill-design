Signing in

Four kinds of people. Four different front doors.

A 78-year-old signing in should face one box and one button. The person who can approve a payout should face considerably more. This is every sign-in and sign-up screen in the platform, and the reasoning behind each.

01 — The approach

Security that scales with what you can do.

The same lock on every door is either too heavy for a family or too light for your accountant. Each group gets the protection its access actually warrants.

WhoHow they get in How they get an accountExtra protection
Patient & family Phone number, then a 6-digit code by SMS Self sign-up, or invited by the family member who books New devices are announced to every other device
Caretaker & field staff Phone number, then a 6-digit code They apply themselves. Your office approves before they can work Sees nothing but their own application until approved
Manager Phone number, then a 6-digit code Created by an Admin. Never self-serve Only ever sees their own area
Admin Phone number, then a 6-digit code Created by the tier above. Never self-serve Confirms again before money moves, and signs out when idle
Super Admin Phone number, then a 6-digit code Set up in person, once, at handover Every action recorded against their name

No passwords anywhere

Nobody has to remember anything. An elderly patient cannot be locked out by a forgotten password, and your office cannot have a password written on a sticky note under a keyboard.

A code never expires on someone slow

Codes last five minutes, and a fresh one can always be sent. Someone who types slowly with a tremor is never locked out — but an automated attack still is.

Signing in never reveals who exists

An unknown number gets exactly the same screen as a registered one. Nobody can use the sign-in page to discover whether a particular person is your patient.

Extra care around money

Signing in is only half of it. Before anyone approves a payout, changes a bank account or exports records in bulk, the platform asks them to confirm it is really them — however recently they signed in. Being logged in is not the same as being present at the keyboard.

The operations panel also signs itself out after fifteen idle minutes, so a screen left open on a desk is not an open door.

Waiting for approvalA limited app, not a locked door

A pending account sees nothing

Until you activate her she can open her own profile, her own documents and her own status. No patients, no staff list, no schedule. An unapproved applicant is a stranger with a form.

You never get half an application

She cannot submit until every document is attached, so your queue holds only complete files ready to be judged. It also stops her waiting on a rejection for something she could have fixed.

Your office sets what she becomes

Nurse or attendant, employed or on contract, which areas she covers — you decide that at approval. It is never a dropdown she picks for herself.

Duplicates are flagged, not blocked

If an application matches an existing one — same ID, same bank account — it is marked for you to look at. Never auto-rejected, because a shared name should not cost someone a job.

Send it back instead of rejecting

A blurred certificate does not need a rejection. Return the application with a note, and she fixes that one item and resubmits.

Abandoned applications clear themselves

An application left untouched for 90 days is deleted. Your queue stays real, and nobody’s documents sit on our servers indefinitely.

06 — The quiet details

What happens when things go wrong.

Most sign-in design is about the unhappy paths. These are the ones that decide whether someone calls you or gives up.

Signing out clears the phone

Everything held on the device — visit notes, queued photos, cached records — is wiped on sign-out. A shared or resold phone keeps nothing.

A lost phone is handled from your office

You can sign out any single device without touching the others. A caretaker who loses her phone keeps working from a new one the same day.

Suspending someone ends their session

The moment your office suspends a staff member, every device they are signed in on stops working. Not at the next sign-in — immediately.

A family member needs the patient’s consent

A son requesting access to his mother’s record cannot be approved by your office alone. She confirms on her own number first.

Permissions are explained before they’re asked

Before the phone asks for location or the camera, one plain screen says why and what happens if refused. Refusing never blocks a visit.

People can leave, and take their data

Deleting an account and downloading a copy of the records are both built in. It is a legal right under India’s data protection law, not a feature.
